# v5 — Design & Findings

A rebuild of the TBT pricing model aimed at one number: **mean absolute
percentage error**.

v4 sits at 8.5% mean / 5.9% median on rolling-origin quarterly validation, and
its own handoff says the algorithmic search is exhausted and the remaining
headroom is in features. That is half right. There is headroom in features, and
v5 goes after it — but there is also headroom in the *objective*, which no
version so far has looked at, and that part is nearly free.

---

## 0. Read this first: what has and has not been measured

**Nothing in this document has been measured against the real archive.**

The archive is customer data and is not in this repository — `*.csv` is
gitignored, deliberately and correctly. Every number below is either carried over
from v4's own measurements, derived analytically, or measured on synthetic data
generated by `tbt5/synth.py`.

Synthetic numbers test the plumbing. They are not accuracy results and must never
be quoted as any. The generator is a guess at how tank pricing works; a model
scored against a guess measures the guess.

The experiment that settles it is one command, and it takes about twenty minutes:

```bash
python bench/compare.py archive_prepared.csv
```

That runs v4 and v5 on identical rows and identical folds and prints a
leaderboard. Until it has been run on the real file, treat this design as a
hypothesis with an argument attached, not as a result.

## 1. Where the remaining error actually is

v4's handoff contains the two facts this version is built on, and reads past both
of them.

**Fact one — the large-tank bias has a named mechanism and an unnamed fix.**

> The ridge backbone alone is **−52%** on this segment. […] Large tanks scale
> **superlinearly** […] Physically that is thicker plate, more shell courses,
> larger crane classes, specialised rigging. None of it is in the 42 available
> columns.

The diagnosis is right. The conclusion — "get plate thickness into the export" —
treats thickness as something TBT measures and we lack. It is not. Thickness is
not measured, it is *designed*, and the design rule is published. See §2.

**Fact two — the headroom table is a model, not a floor.**

> | Conditioned on | median | mean |
> |---|---|---|
> | Identical spec | 5.9% | 18.3% |
> | + state | 2.2% | 6.3% |
> | + state + wage type + year | 1.5% | **4.6%** |

This is presented as the noise floor. But "find rows matching on spec, state,
wage and year and look at their price" is not a description of noise. It is a
description of a nearest-neighbour model, and the table says such a model lands
near 4.6% where the shipped gradient booster lands at 8.7%. See §4.

And one fact v4 records without following:

> **Absolute-error loss** — better p90, notably worse aggregate bias.

Rejected on aggregate bias. But the objective here is mean APE, and absolute loss
in log space is the loss that matches it. See §3.

## 2. Thesis one — compute the shell schedule instead of waiting for it

### The functional-form problem

v4's parametric backbone is a ridge in `[log D, log H, log D × log H, t]`. That
is a smooth power law. The true cost curve is not smooth, and the reason is a
table in API 650 §5.6.1.1 — the minimum nominal shell thickness:

| Diameter | Minimum shell thickness |
|---|---|
| < 50 ft | 3/16 in |
| 50 – 120 ft | 1/4 in |
| 120 – 200 ft | 5/16 in |
| ≥ 200 ft | 3/8 in |

Below the governing diameter, the minimum binds. Every course is 3/16" whatever
the head, steel weight tracks surface area, and cost goes as `D·H`.

Above it, the one-foot method binds — `t ∝ D·H` — so steel weight goes as
`D·H·t ∝ D²·H`, and cost with it.

That is a **kink at a fixed physical threshold**. No power law in `D` and `H` can
represent a kink, at any coefficients. It is not a matter of tuning. This is why
v4's backbone reads −52% on the large segment: it is the wrong shape, and the
trees are then left to learn the regime change from scratch in the thin tail of
the data where they have the fewest rows to learn it from.

### What the computation gives you

`tbt5/physics.py` lays out courses from the bottom up, designs each for the head
one foot above its own base, applies the minimum-thickness table, rounds to 1/16"
plate, and sums the weight. Over a representative spread:

| D (ft) | H (ft) | shell area | t at base | min-governed | courses | steel lb | **lb/sq-ft** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 20 | 16 | 1,005 | 0.188 | 100% | 2 | 7,697 | **7.66** |
| 32 | 30 | 3,016 | 0.188 | 100% | 4 | 23,091 | **7.66** |
| 48 | 40 | 6,032 | 0.312 | 40% | 5 | 61,575 | **10.21** |
| 60 | 40 | 7,540 | 0.375 | 60% | 5 | 88,514 | **11.74** |
| 90 | 50 | 14,137 | 0.688 | 29% | 7 | 253,998 | **17.97** |
| 111 | 51 | 17,785 | 0.812 | 29% | 7 | 373,781 | **21.02** |
| 150 | 60 | 28,274 | 1.250 | 25% | 8 | 880,333 | **31.14** |
| 200 | 60 | 37,699 | 1.625 | 25% | 8 | 1,500,896 | **39.81** |

Steel intensity is flat at 7.66 lb/sq-ft across the whole minimum-governed range
and then climbs to 39.8 — a **5.2× spread that surface area cannot see**.

The 111 × 51 ft tank in that table is not chosen at random. It is the one v2
priced at $1.5M against an actual $3.1M. It carries **2.7× the steel per square
foot** of a small tank. That factor is the missing superlinear term, and it is
computable from two columns we already have.

### What goes into the model

`features.backbone` becomes a ridge in

```
[log D, log H, log D × log H, log(steel_lb), log(steel_lb) × min_gov, min_gov, t]
```

which **nests v4's basis** — if the physics terms are worthless the ridge zeroes
them and v5's backbone degenerates to v4's. The `log(steel) × min_gov`
interaction is what lets the slope differ between regimes, which is the kink.

Twenty-two more physics features go to the trees: course count, distinct plate
thicknesses in the schedule, wind-girder count from the API 650 unstiffened-height
formula, roof column count, crane lift class, a seismic overturning proxy.

### The honest caveat

These are code-*shaped* proxies, not certified design calculations. The
constants are representative; real designs carry corrosion allowances, annular
rings, nozzle reinforcement and mill plate availability that we cannot see.

That is fine, and the reason it is fine is worth stating: a monotone transform of
a feature is free to a tree, so being wrong by a constant factor costs nothing.
Being wrong by a *functional form* is what costs 13–17%. This fixes the form.

Nothing in `physics.py` should be quoted to a customer or used as an engineering
result.

## 3. Thesis two — optimise the metric you are actually judged on

Every version so far fitted `log(price)` under squared loss and exponentiated.
That predicts the conditional geometric mean. It is not the estimate that
minimises mean APE.

### The derivation

For a point estimate `z` against an outcome `Y`, minimise `E[|z − Y| / Y]`:

```
d/dz E[|z − Y|/Y] = E[ sign(z − Y) / Y ] = 0
```

The minimiser is the median of `Y` under the measure reweighted by `1/y`. Not the
mean, not the median, and always **below** the median when price is right-skewed.

The reason is that APE is asymmetric. Overpredicting by 2× costs 100%;
underpredicting by 2× costs 50%. A metric that punishes overprediction twice as
hard is minimised by an estimate that leans low.

For a lognormal conditional distribution this is closed form. If
`log Y ~ N(μ, σ²)` then the reweighted measure is lognormal with location
`μ − σ²`, so

```
z* = exp(μ − σ²)
```

The shrink is proportional to the **conditional variance**. That is the part that
matters, and the part a flat recalibration cannot reproduce: it shrinks hard
exactly where the model is unsure — big tanks, thin segments, unusual scope — and
barely at all where it is confident.

This also explains why v4's recalibration attempts moved nothing. Isotonic and
log-log recalibration both search for a *bias* to correct, and v4 correctly
reports there is none (log-log slope 1.007). There is no bias. There is a
mismatch between the loss that was fitted and the metric that is reported, and no
calibrator that maps prediction to prediction can see it.

### Implementation

`LogPriceModel` fits quantile heads at 10/25/50/75/90 on the residual.
`decision.mape_optimal` reconstructs the conditional distribution — interpolating
in the normal score, so tails extend rather than flatten — and takes the
1/y-weighted median numerically, which respects genuine skew rather than assuming
lognormality. `decision.fit_global_shift` then fits one scalar multiplier
out-of-fold to mop up whatever is left.

### Three objectives, because the trade is real

An estimator tuned for mean APE is deliberately low, so summing its predictions
undershoots. That is correct for per-quote work and wrong for valuing a backlog.
So the decision rule is a parameter:

| `objective` | Returns | Use it for |
|---|---|---|
| `mape` (default) | 1/y-weighted median | per-quote accuracy — the headline metric |
| `median` | conditional median | when median error is what matters |
| `unbiased` | conditional mean | anything that gets **summed** — backlog, portfolio |

v4's handoff says "do not use it to value a backlog". With `objective="unbiased"`
that warning is addressed rather than inherited.

Measured on synthetic data, the ordering comes out exactly as the algebra
predicts — `mape` lowest on mean APE, `unbiased` best on aggregate bias:

| objective | mean | median | p90 | $agg | $top5% |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| median | 5.25% | 5.15% | 10.9% | +0.8% | −4.2% |
| **mape** | **5.06%** | 4.84% | **9.4%** | −0.2% | −5.7% |
| unbiased | 5.42% | 4.90% | 11.2% | **+1.3%** | **−3.4%** |

Synthetic data. This shows the machinery does what it says, not how much it is
worth on the archive.

### Losses

All boosting uses `absolute_error` rather than `squared_error`. For small errors
`APE ≈ |log ŷ − log y|`, so absolute loss in log space is a first-order match to
the objective. Squared loss chases the few quotes with the largest log residuals
at the expense of the many.

v4 measured this as "better p90, notably worse aggregate bias" and rejected it on
the bias. Under an explicit mean-APE objective, that is the right trade to take —
and `objective="unbiased"` recovers the aggregate case separately.

Incidental finding: on this machine `absolute_error` is not slower than
`squared_error` for HistGradientBoosting — 17.9s against 130s for 1,200
iterations on 6,578 × 85. Do not avoid it on cost grounds.

## 4. Thesis three — build the model the headroom table describes

`tbt5/comparables.py` is a distance-weighted kNN over log $/sq-ft: size, scope,
material, use type, wage regime, geography, with a Gaussian kernel whose
bandwidth is set by the neighbourhood itself.

Two rules make it a model rather than a leak:

1. **Strict causality.** A row may only be compared against rows dated before it.
   `fit_transform_causal` walks forward a month at a time, rebuilding the
   reference index each step. It is slower than one global index and it is the
   only reason the output means anything.
2. **No self-quotes.** 6,890 rows are 3,018 quotes. Letting revision 3 find
   revision 2 as its comparable reports about 3% error and predicts nothing.
   Same-`Quote #` rows are excluded from every neighbour set.

The output is fed to the booster as **features**, not blended in at a fixed
weight — `cmp_logpsf`, `cmp_n`, `cmp_dist`, `cmp_spread`. That lets the tree learn
*when* to trust the analogy: a thin or self-contradictory neighbourhood is
exactly the signal needed to discount it. The comparables prediction also stands
as a third blend entrant in its own right.

**On synthetic data it contributes nothing** — 13.6% standalone, blend weight
0.00. That is an honest negative and it is what the generator predicts: a smooth
deterministic cost process is precisely what a booster fits best and what
analogy adds least to. Whether it earns its place on the archive is an open
question, and `bench/compare.py` has an entrant with it switched off so the
question gets answered rather than assumed.

## 5. Smaller changes, each with a reason

### Quote-level deduplication weighting

6,890 rows are 3,018 quotes, because a revision is stored as another row. A quote
revised eight times therefore carries eight times the training weight of one
accepted first time — and revision count correlates with how contested and
unusual a job is. The fit tilts toward exactly the rows whose pricing is least
representative.

v4 treats the revision structure purely as a leakage hazard to split around. It
is also a weighting bug. `data.weights` divides each quote's weight among its
revisions, so every quote contributes one unit.

### Blend weights fitted rather than fixed

v4 blends component-sum and direct at a hardcoded 70/30, noting that anything
between 0.3 and 0.8 lands within 0.2 points. True for two learners that fail in
similar places; not something to assume for three that do not. `fit_blend`
searches the simplex on a coarse grid, scored on mean APE, on a trailing inner
holdout. The grid is coarse on purpose — the surface is flat, and a fine search
would be fitting noise.

Learners that abstain (comparables, when nothing close enough exists) are
renormalised away on those rows rather than substituted with a default.

### Robustness: dropped degenerate columns

v4 crashes on an export missing an optional numeric column outright —
HistGradientBoosting's binner raises `window shape cannot be larger than input
array shape` on an all-NaN feature. `Miles to Site (From GT)` is exactly this
case. v5's `Encoder` drops all-missing and constant numeric columns at fit time
and keeps them dropped at score time.

## 6. What is carried over from v4 unchanged, and why

These are not defaults. They are conclusions that were paid for, and re-deriving
them would be waste:

| Inherited | Why it stays |
|---|---|
| **Scope is an input, never inferred** | A classifier here tests beautifully (AUC 0.91–0.99) and fails silently — a confidently wrong scope changes the price with nothing visible on the sheet. `ScopeError` is a feature. |
| **`IS_INSULATION_ERECTION` implies `IS_INSULATION`** | 1,456 archive rows have both, 83 supply-only, zero erection-alone. |
| **`IS_TAXABLE` is not derivable from `State`** | Geography sets the rate; customer exemption sets whether it applies. Oregon 0%, Texas 38%, New Jersey 91%. |
| **Never divide prices by `Quantity`** | They are already per-tank. v1 and v2 both did and corrupted 15% of rows — 38% of the top 5% by value. |
| **Filter implausible rows out of *training*** | v2 filtered scoring only, which measures the model more kindly without improving it. |
| **Rolling origin by quarter, never a random split** | A random split leaks revisions and reports ~3% instead of ~8%. |
| **Recency weighting, 1-year half-life** | Measured best of several in v3. |
| **A parametric backbone under the trees** | Trees predict a constant beyond their largest leaf. This is what lets the model extrapolate. |
| **Tax is a state-rate lookup, never modelled** | It is arithmetic. |
| **The `BANNED` list** | Component prices are identities with the target; margin/contingency/commission are pricing-policy decisions made during quoting, not tank attributes. |
| **Retrain monthly** | Worth 1.3 points — more than every modelling change in v4 combined, for two minutes of CPU. |

That last row is worth repeating, because it outranks everything in this
document. **If only one thing gets done, schedule the retrain.**

## 7. What v5 costs

| | v4 | v5 |
|---|---|---|
| Fitted estimators | 14 | 13 + 4 quantile heads + a kNN index |
| Training time | ~2 min (claimed) | ~2.5 min, calibration pass included |
| Dependencies | +none | +scipy |
| Extra complexity | — | a decision layer and a causal kNN |

The calibration pass runs at half the boosting budget and drops the second GBM
variant (`light=True`). Its outputs are three blend weights on a grid of 21, one
scalar shift and two band multipliers — none of which move on the last few
hundred boosting rounds. Keeping the retrain inside two minutes matters more than
the last hundredth of a point: a retrain nobody schedules because it takes twenty
minutes is worth nothing, and cadence is worth 1.3 points.

## 8. What would falsify each thesis

Stated in advance, so the bench run is a test rather than a search for
confirmation:

| Thesis | Falsified if |
|---|---|
| Physics features fix the large-tank bias | `$top5%` on the real archive stays near −13% to −17% |
| The physics backbone is the right *shape* | The ridge coefficients on `log(steel)` and its interaction come out near zero |
| The MAPE decision layer is worth having | `objective="mape"` does not beat `objective="median"` on mean APE |
| Comparables are worth having | The `no physics/comparables` entrant matches the full model |
| Dedup weighting helps | Turning it off (`data.weights(dedup=False)`) does not raise mean APE |

If the first two fail together, the conclusion is that TBT's prices are not
driven by steel weight the way the design codes suggest — which is itself worth
knowing, and points at labour and rigging rather than materials.

## 9. Open problems

**Win-rate modelling is still impossible.** `Status` is Won 173, Lost 142 out of
6,892. Under 5% resolved. No amount of modelling fixes 315 labelled examples
across 33 countries and 14 use types. It needs outcome capture on a meaningful
share of quotes first — a process change. Say so early and plainly: "AI pricing
model" gets heard as "tells us what wins", and the gap between that and what this
does is where trust gets destroyed.

**Scope should be captured at quote time.** `prepare_data.py` backfills scope by
asking whether a component carries a non-zero price. That is the right migration
for history and the wrong thing to rely on going forward: a blank price and a
genuinely excluded scope are indistinguishable after the fact. Five Yes/No
dropdowns, and they are the same five answers the model needs anyway.

**Argentina.** 116 rows, 38.7% error and −16.7% bias under v1, never re-checked.
Treat AR output with suspicion until someone does. `cli.py validate --segments`
prints the per-country breakdown.

**The physics layer could be checked directly.** Every claim in §2 is falsifiable
by an engineer in an afternoon: take twenty quotes, compare `ph_shell_lb` against
the actual shell weight on the drawings, and regress. If the correlation is
strong the feature is doing what it claims. If it is weak, the constants need
work — and that is a much smaller problem than the one v4 handed forward.
